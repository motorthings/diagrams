What this is, if you've never heard of any of it

Law firms are all rushing to use artificial intelligence right now, because it can draft a document or dig through research in seconds when the same work used to take a junior lawyer half the night, and the pull to lean on it is enormous. The trouble is that nobody has fully settled which of the mistakes AI makes will actually get a lawyer in real trouble, and the rules that will eventually govern all of it are still being written, one court case and one ethics ruling at a time. So every firm is quietly making a bet, whether they'll admit it or not, about what the rules are going to say before those rules actually exist.

This is a tool for making that bet with your eyes open. It watches the places where something new that AI can do bumps up against an old rule about how lawyers are supposed to behave, and it tries to tell you which of those collisions the legal world is about to crack down on next. It isn't predicting a date and it isn't promising anything for certain. It measures pressure, the way a weather service tracks a storm building out over the ocean, so a firm can get ready before the thing makes landfall instead of scrambling after it already has.

The way it does that is deliberately unglamorous. Every week it reads a wide pile of real, dated, sourced material: court punishments handed to lawyers, ethics opinions, new laws, what the software companies are building, what the insurance companies are starting to demand before they'll cover a firm. And it flatly refuses to be impressed by noise, so a ruling from an actual court counts for a great deal while a marketing blog from a company selling AI counts for almost nothing, which means ten breathless blog posts can never outweigh one real decision. That single rule is what keeps the whole thing honest.

Before any fact is allowed to count for anything, three separate checkers each try to prove it wrong, and it only gets in if it survives at least two of them, which is exactly how the system has already caught its own would-be mistakes, like a court rule that only one website ever mentioned and a claim about two companies that fell apart the moment someone looked closely. And the part I'm proudest of is that the tool goes back and grades its own old predictions against what actually happened, so you are never asked to believe it just because it sounds sure of itself. You can check whether it was right.

Everything below this point is the detail, the specific collisions it tracks, the way it scores each one, and where all the evidence comes from. If this is as far as you read, that's genuinely fine. The whole thing exists to answer one honest question, which is where the law around AI is going to move next, and to show its work while it guesses.

Part 1

What it is

The radar forecasts where legal AI is headed by tracking fault lines — the places a new AI capability rubs against an existing legal duty. It does not predict dates. It measures pressure, weighted by who is doing the pushing.

Part 3

How it scores

Authority over volume. Every piece of evidence is stamped with a tier when it enters, and the tier decides the weight — not how many people repeated it.

TierWhat it isWeight
T1Court ruling, statute1.00
T2Bar opinion, regulatory guidance0.80
T3Empirical study, institution0.60
T4Commentary, trade press0.35
T5Vendor marketing0.008

A vendor blog carries about a hundredth of a bar opinion. Ten vendor posts cannot outweigh one ruling. Two things keep the scoring honest: the L2 ruling meter is two-sided (an item can argue against a line and drag it down, not just up), and every capital event is flagged continuation / origination / direction-shift — the momentum signal only predicts continuation, so a first-of-kind move is labeled rather than scored as if it were predictable.

Part 4

How evidence gets in

A fact does not enter the feed because someone found it. It enters because it survived being argued against.

A fact is found on the internet
↓
Split the question, search it from several directions at once — including one angle that looks for where the story is wrong
↓
Three independent reviewers each try to prove it false
↓
Does it survive 2 of 3 skeptics?
↓
Admitted and ranked · or killed — and if a date or amount cannot be verified, the honest answer is "we found nothing," not a guessed number

This is the step that caught real mistakes: a court rule only one source mentioned, a "disqualification" that was actually a reprimand, a claim about two vendors no source could hold up.

Seventy-six dated, cited items flow from four streams into eleven fault-line predictions, ranked by queue score. Each prediction's bar is built out of its streams — one colour means one source agrees, four colours means the engine is sure. Full interactive version: the streams page.

Bar length scales with the queue score (0–10); each colour band is one stream's share of that call. Ribbon width is an item's mass split across the calls it serves, not a head-count — one item can feed several fault lines. Hover a stream or a prediction to trace where its evidence lands.

Part 6

What to watch next

The open signal

No insurer has yet made a certified AI tool a condition of malpractice coverage, and no bar has adopted a named benchmark. When one does, that is the event that flips the method layer from voluntary to enforceable. That absence — the thing that has not happened yet — is the single most valuable signal in the whole system.
