What is the Fault-Line Radar?

Law firms are rushing to use artificial intelligence, because it drafts and researches in seconds what used to take a junior lawyer half the night, and the pull to lean on it is enormous. Nobody has settled which of the mistakes AI makes will actually get a lawyer in real trouble, and the rules that will govern it are still being written, one court case and one ethics ruling at a time. So every firm is quietly betting on what the rules will say before those rules exist.

This is a tool for making that bet with your eyes open. It watches where something new that AI can do bumps against an old rule about how lawyers must behave, and tells you which collision the legal world is about to crack down on next. It measures pressure, the way a weather service tracks a storm building at sea, so a firm gets ready before landfall instead of scrambling after it.

Every week it reads a wide pile of real, dated, sourced material: court punishments, ethics opinions, new laws, what the software companies are building, what insurers demand before they'll cover a firm. A ruling from an actual court counts for a great deal; a marketing blog counts for almost nothing, so ten breathless blog posts can never outweigh one real decision.

Before any fact counts for anything, three separate checkers try to prove it wrong, and it only gets in if it survives at least two of them. That is how the system has already caught its own mistakes, like a court rule that only one website mentioned and a claim about two companies that fell apart the moment someone looked closely. It also goes back and grades its own past readings against what actually happened.

The tool comes in three parts, and each answers a different question.

Part A

Scoring core

Will a court, bar, or law move on this?
Graded against what actually happened. This is the part that earns trust by being scored, not by sounding sure.
Part B

Advisory layer

What should a firm build and buy next?
Turns Part A's readouts into build-and-buy advice. Evidence-traced, never framed as a call.
Part C

Firm simulation

Can this firm afford to act?
A what-if model of one firm's economics. Its headline: on hourly billing, more AI is a net loss; on fixed fee, it's a net win.

One fault line, end to end

Here is an example, worked all the way through.

A real fault line, end to end

A lawyer has a hard duty to keep client secrets private. An AI that reads a firm's documents can now do the work in seconds. Type the secrets in, though, and they quietly belong to the company running the tool. Secrecy against speed. That collision is a fault line.

The pressure on it is already high, because the duty is absolute while the tools are new and loose. What would settle it is the law making a move. Until then the honest read is pressure building, and the fix a firm can stand up today is data-flow mapping and vendor attestation, so a lawyer knows where the secrets go before they go anywhere.

The evidence: four kinds of authority

A reading is only as good as the evidence under it, so the radar refuses to lean on any single source. Every item that counts is one of four kinds of authority, and the kinds are kept apart because they are not interchangeable: a circuit court can sanction a lawyer, and a bar association can discipline one, but a state ethics opinion carries a different weight from a ruling that binds.

Every item is real, dated, and traceable, and weighted by how much authority it carries before it counts. A court ruling moves a reading far more than a company's press release ever could. A reading is the sum of its evidence, collected, weighted, and added up.

Appellate court20 sources

Circuit courts and state appellate benches. When a higher court rules, the reading moves further than it does for anything below it.

Trial court11 sources

District orders and sanctions. Lower authority, but this is where enforcement usually starts, so it moves early.

Statute or rule22 sources

Binding primary law that needs no court to decide anything. A statute binds without any AI capability driver behind it, which is why a line can carry a high ruling reading and a low capability one.

Bar guidance30 sources

Ethics opinions and bar guidance. The profession telling itself what is required. Counted, but never weighted as if it were a ruling.

What counts as authority

Authority over volume, set by one test: how much can this speaker actually do to a lawyer? A court can sanction you. A bar can discipline you. An insurer can refuse to cover you. A vendor can only sell to you. Every item is stamped with its tier when it enters, and the tier decides the weight, not how many people repeated it.

A stream and a tier answer different questions: a stream is the topic, a tier is how much power the speaker holds. Most rules-and-rulings items are high authority and most vendor items are low, but the radar keeps the two apart so a low-authority claim cannot borrow a prestigious name.

How it decides: the five meters

Every fault line is read on five meters at once. Three watch the supply side, the chain of things that has to exist before a firm can act. Two watch the governance side, whether the law and the market are about to force the issue. They do not all fire in that order, and the board does not pretend they do: convergence and vendor liability both carry a ruling reading above 8 with a capability reading at 2.5, because a statute binds without any capability driver behind it.

How a reading becomes an instruction

Everything above is what the record says, and it is true of every firm. This part depends on who you are, and it is where the radar stops describing and starts advising. Two questions are asked of every control, and they are never blended into one answer.

Govern

Do you have to do this?

Answered from the law and the market, never from the economics. A required control is not deferred because the AI math is poor: the billable hour does not excuse a firm from a competence program it is required to have.
Deploy

Does it pay to put AI on the work?

A separate decision with a separate answer. A rule requiring something is not a reason to put AI on it, and on hourly billing more AI is usually a net loss whatever the rule says.
The line between them

“Have to” comes in two kinds

A duty is something the law already requires: comply, or risk being sanctioned. A market norm is something your peers have started doing: match it, or lose work. The two are labelled separately and never merged.

A norm only counts if someone with something to withhold requires it. An insurer who can drop your coverage. A client who can move the engagement. A platform that can withhold the docket. A high adoption score on its own is not enough, and that is deliberate: it is why the model does not use one.

Leverage is firm-relative. A carrier you name yourself clears the evidence bar by itself, because one carrier requiring something of you is a stronger signal than two requiring it of the market in general. Naming your carrier is what turns a thin norm into a supported one.

What to watch next

The open signal

Carriers have started asking. CNA added AI-governance questions to malpractice renewals and underwriting in June 2026, which is a questionnaire rather than a condition, and no bar has yet adopted a named benchmark.

The event to watch is the step past asking: an insurer naming a certified tool as a condition of coverage. That is what flips the method from voluntary to enforceable.

The most valuable signal in the whole system is the thing that has not happened yet.
