What this is, if you've never heard of any of it

Law firms are rushing to use artificial intelligence, because it drafts and researches in seconds what used to take a junior lawyer half the night, and the pull to lean on it is enormous. Nobody has settled which of the mistakes AI makes will actually get a lawyer in real trouble, and the rules that will govern it are still being written, one court case and one ethics ruling at a time. So every firm is quietly betting on what the rules will say before those rules exist.

This is a tool for making that bet with your eyes open. It watches where something new that AI can do bumps against an old rule about how lawyers must behave, and tells you which collision the legal world is about to crack down on next. It does not predict a date and promises nothing. It measures pressure, the way a weather service tracks a storm building at sea, so a firm gets ready before landfall instead of scrambling after it.

The way it does that is deliberately unglamorous. Every week it reads a wide pile of real, dated, sourced material: court punishments, ethics opinions, new laws, what the software companies are building, what insurers demand before they'll cover a firm. And it flatly refuses to be impressed by noise. A ruling from an actual court counts for a great deal; a marketing blog counts for almost nothing, so ten breathless blog posts can never outweigh one real decision. That single rule keeps the whole thing honest.

Before any fact counts for anything, three separate checkers try to prove it wrong, and it only gets in if it survives at least two of them. That is how the system has already caught its own mistakes, like a court rule that only one website mentioned and a claim about two companies that fell apart the moment someone looked closely.

And the part I'm proudest of is that the tool goes back and grades its own old predictions against what actually happened, so you are never asked to believe it just because it sounds sure of itself. You can check whether it was right.

Everything below is the detail: the specific collisions it tracks, how it scores each one, where the evidence comes from. If this is as far as you read, that's fine. The whole thing exists to answer one honest question, where the law around AI moves next, and to show its work while it guesses.

Part 1

What a fault line is

The radar forecasts where legal AI is headed by tracking fault lines, the places where something new that AI can do rubs against an old rule about how lawyers must behave. It does not predict dates. It measures pressure, weighted by who is doing the pushing.

A real fault line, end to end

A lawyer has a hard, ancient duty to keep client secrets private. An AI that reads a firm's documents can now do the work in seconds. Type the secrets in, though, and they quietly belong to the company running the tool. Secrecy against speed. That collision is a fault line.

The pressure on it is already high, because the duty is absolute while the tools are new and loose. What would settle it is the law making a move. Until then the honest forecast is pressure building, and the fix a firm can stand up today is data-flow mapping and vendor attestation, so a lawyer knows where the secrets go before they go anywhere.

Reading eleven of these at once takes a tool built in three parts, and each part answers a different question.

Part A

Forecast core

Will a court, bar, or law move on this?
Graded against what actually happened. This is the part that earns trust by being scored, not by sounding sure.
Part B

Advisory layer

What should a firm build and buy next?
Turns Part A's readouts into build-and-buy advice. Evidence-traced, never framed as a prediction.
Part C

Firm simulation

Can this firm afford to act?
A what-if model of one firm's economics. Its headline: on hourly billing, more AI is a net loss; on fixed fee, it's a net win.
Part 2

The evidence, where the facts come from

This is the part everything else rests on. A prediction is only as good as the evidence under it, so the radar refuses to lean on any single source. It watches four different kinds of evidence as four separate witnesses to one question: whether a duty around legal AI is about to become real. When only one witness is talking it stays cautious; when all four point the same way, it starts to believe the call.

Every item is real, dated, and traceable, and weighted by how much authority it carries before it counts. A court ruling moves a prediction far more than a company's press release ever could. Where a prediction lands on the queue is just the sum of its evidence, collected, weighted, and added up. The streams are where that evidence comes from, which is why this section is the heart of the system, not a picture of it.

Rules & rulings25 items

Court sanctions, ethics opinions, and new statutes. What the people who can actually punish a lawyer are deciding.

Vendors24 items

Funding rounds, acquisitions, and product launches. What the companies selling legal AI are betting real money on.

Capability17 items

Model releases, benchmarks, and studies. What the technology can genuinely do yet, separate from the marketing.

Market & insurers10 items

Insurer questionnaires, client demands, and adoption. What the people paying for all of it are starting to require.

Part 3

What counts as authority

Authority over volume, set by one test: how much can this speaker actually do to a lawyer? A court can sanction you. A bar can discipline you. An insurer can refuse to cover you. A vendor can only sell to you. So one ruling outweighs ten blog posts, not because it is louder but because the person behind it holds real power over you. Every item is stamped with its tier when it enters, and the tier decides the weight, not how many people repeated it.

TierWhat it isWeight

A stream and a tier answer different questions: a stream is the topic, a tier is how much power the speaker holds. Most rules-and-rulings items are high authority and most vendor items are low, but the radar keeps the two apart so a low-authority claim cannot borrow a prestigious name.

Part 5

How it decides, the five meters

Every fault line is read on five meters at once, and the five reads fold into the single queue number that decides where the line ranks. Three watch the supply side, the chain of things that has to exist before a firm can act. Two watch the governance side, whether the law and the market are about to force the issue.

The supply stack — can AI do it, can vendors ship it, can a firm adopt it
L1 capabilitycan the AI do the thing yet
S softwareare legal-AI vendors enabled to build it
E enablementcan a firm stand the fix up with what the market sells
The governance side — will the law move, and will the fix become standard practice
L2 rulingwill a court, bar, or statute move on it
L3 adoptionis the fix becoming standard practice, court or no court

Two things here share the name "capability," as two lenses on one question. Capability the meter asks whether the AI can do the thing. The capability stream from Part 2 is the evidence that answers it, the model releases, benchmarks, and studies. The meter is the read; the stream is the source.

Two rules keep the numbers honest. The ruling meter is two-sided: an item can argue against a line and drag it down, not just push it up. And every capital event is flagged continuation, origination, or a direction shift, because momentum can only predict continuation; a first-of-its-kind move gets labelled honestly rather than scored as if it were predictable.

Part 7

What to watch next

The open signal

No insurer has yet made a certified AI tool a condition of malpractice coverage, and no bar has adopted a named benchmark. When one does, the method flips from voluntary to enforceable. That absence is the single most valuable signal in the whole system.
