How I work · one problem, start to finish

Legal AI is moving too fast to guess.

So I built something that doesn't have to. This is the problem I chased and the way I chased it, and the Fault-Line Radar is the thing that came out the other end.

The problem

The field is moving faster than anyone can track, and the effects that actually decide how a firm operates sit two and three orders downstream.

First-order changes are already hard to follow. The second- and third-order effects rippling out behind them are where the real consequences land, out where almost nobody has any real visibility at all.

Can you see across those order-effects clearly enough to predict where AI in legal practice is genuinely headed, and build a model that makes those calls with honest, checkable confidence?

It started the way everything I build starts, with the flip from “wouldn't it be nice if we could see this coming” to “how would I build the thing that sees it.”

What follows is how I work, shown on one real problem instead of described.
How I built it

The same lifecycle my AESOP platform runs teams through, here on one problem: discover, design, build, evaluate, repair, track.

Discover→ Design→ Build→ Evaluate→ Repair→ Track
1
Discover

Keep the problem big, and hold off on “how.”

Discovery is where I refuse to shrink the problem, digging for the root instead of the symptom and keeping feasibility out of the room, because deciding too early what's possible only narrows what you let yourself imagine. And the shape of this one mattered: the ripples sort into three orders, and the model has to see all three, whether the tech can do it (first order), whether the law and the bar react (second order), and what the market is then forced to adopt (third order), each order resting on the one before it, and that third order, the one about what firms actually have to buy and build, is where it gets genuinely interesting.

2
Design

Let the constraints pick the stack

Then I press the problem against the solution space until the constraints fall out, because the constraints are what pick the stack for you: it had to be deterministic and replayable, weighted by who sets the rules rather than by volume, gradeable against what actually happened, and auditable after the fact. Those demands ruled out letting a model freehand the forecast and pointed straight at plain scored code, and with that settled the rest of the forks resolve fast:

DecisionOptionsChosenWhy
Generate the forecast LLM free-generation vs deterministic weighted scoring Deterministic scoring Replayable and auditable. No hallucinated confidence.
Weight the sources By volume vs by authority tier Authority tiers T1 binding at 1.0 down to T5 vendor at 0.008. Who sets the rule matters, not how loud.
Grade the calls Predict dates vs called-versus-actual at 90-day lead Called-versus-actual Checkable against reality. A miss reads as a miss.
Prediction depth Single ruling meter vs three-order cascade Cascade Capability → ruling → adoption. Each layer only counts if the one it rests on held.
Conflicting evidence Average it out vs discount on conflict Conflict discount Disagreement should lower confidence, not wash out.
3

AI for leverage, the logic stays mine

4
Evaluate

Grade it against reality, then say what broke

5
Repair

A logged miss is the next work item

A miss isn't something to bury, it's the next thing to build, and this one points straight at the fix: the cascade is thin at the bottom, so the next pass adds a graded capability lane and widens the market evidence the adoption calls run on.

6
Track

Explainable, auditable, traceable

Every part of the run gets logged, the inputs and the reasoning and the output, so the whole thing audits end to end and a problem gets caught while it's still just an issue. Every run is hashed and replayable, every score carries the evidence and math that produced it, and every call traces back to the sources it rested on, so one call is a guess but a tracked record is a track record.

The same record is where the improvements come from, because once I can watch how the tool actually gets used and how other people push on it, the gaps and the rough edges surface on their own, and the loop reopens with real evidence instead of a guess.

The lawyers own the doctrine. I bring the modeling, the calibration, and the discipline of checking my own work and asking where I'm wrong. Safety lives in every one of those steps, in the source weights and the audit trail and the misses I leave on the page, and it has to, because a forecast no one can read is a forecast no one can trust.
