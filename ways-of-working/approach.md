# The Approach — Before You Add Anything

There's a version of this that goes badly. You install Claude Code, spend a week adding every MCP and skill you saw someone post about on LinkedIn, end up with a slow and cluttered setup you don't fully understand, and quietly stop using it. That's not a failure of the tool. It's a failure of approach.

This is the approach.

---

## Mindset 1: not "if," but "how"

Most of the process improvements you've been sitting on aren't impossible. They're just unsolved.

The question is almost never whether AI can help with something. It's which approach, which combination of tools, which framing gets you there. Coming in with "can this even be done?" keeps you in the shallow end — you're looking for permission instead of a path. Coming in with "how do I do this?" is where the real work starts and where the leverage is.

This matters because the gap between what people think AI can do for them and what it actually can do is enormous — and almost always in the direction of more, not less. The constraint is rarely capability. It's imagination and approach.

So when you hit a friction point in your work, the move is not to wonder whether there's a solution. Assume there is. Then ask Claude how to get there.

---

## Mindset 2: the internet is lying to you (a little)

Every week someone posts about a tool or workflow that changed everything for them. Most of those posts are true: for that person, in their context, doing their kind of work.

A lot of what gets shared is built for developers. Tools for managing code, running tests, deploying software, navigating large repos. If you're not doing those things, those tools aren't for you. Not because they're bad. They just don't match the work. Installing them anyway doesn't make you a power user. It makes your setup slower and harder to understand.

The question is never "is this impressive?" The question is always "does this match how I actually work?"

---

## Mindset 3: shiny object syndrome has a real cost

Every tool you add takes something. MCPs load into your context at the start of every session, whether you use them or not. Skills add decision overhead. Config files get longer. The more you add, the more Claude has to sort through before it gets to your actual question.

A lean setup you understand will outperform an impressive setup you don't. Every time.

The chaos creeps in gradually. One MCP here, one skill there, something someone shared that seemed useful. None of it feels like a big deal in the moment. And then one day you notice Claude is slower, your sessions feel heavier, and you're not sure what half your config actually does. That's bloat. Avoid it before it starts.

---

## Mindset 4: the filter is asking Claude

Here's what makes this different from every other tool you've tried to adopt: Claude has context on you. Your files, your meeting notes, your projects, the way you write and think and work: it's all there. It's building a picture of you every session.

So before you add anything, ask it:

> *"Based on what you know about how I work and what I spend my time on, would [this thing] actually help me? If not, what's in that space that might?"*

That question does something most people skip. It forces the evaluation to be about your work, not about the tool's marketing. Claude can look at your notes, see what you do repeatedly, and tell you whether a given skill or MCP addresses something real or just sounds good.

You're not asking "is this a good tool?" You're asking "is this a good tool for me, specifically, given what I actually do?"

---

## Mindset 5: friction is the signal

A few questions worth running before you install anything:

**Do I do this repeatedly?** If you've hit the same friction point three or more times and it slowed you down, that's a signal. If you've never actually run into the problem, it's not.

**Is this for my kind of work?** A lot of tools are built for engineers, researchers, or people managing large codebases. If your work is meetings, documents, Jira, and Glean — that's the lens.

**What would I actually do differently if I had it?** If you can't name a specific thing you'd do differently tomorrow, that's a sign you're adding it on hope rather than need.

**Have I tried doing it without the tool first?** Asking Claude directly — without any special skill or MCP — often works better than you'd expect. Add infrastructure when the direct approach breaks down, not before.

---

## The rule

Start with nothing. Add one thing when you hit friction. Use it for a week. If it's not improving how you work, remove it.

Repeat.

The goal is a setup that feels invisible — where the tools are so well-matched to your work that you stop thinking about the tools and just think about the work.

That's the whole game.
