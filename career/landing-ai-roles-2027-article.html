A Person Who Actually Exists

By Charlie Fuller · companion to the one-page field briefing

I put in two hundred and three applications between May and July. Most of them ended in silence. Three turned into offers, and two of those got pulled back when the companies reorganized. One extended an offer, told me three days later not to resign anything yet, and pulled it two weeks later. The other ran me through the CEO and the COO and the CTO, said yes, and took it back five days after. The one that held came through a LinkedIn message I never sent anything for.

Both of those companies reorganized in the middle of hiring me. Companies large and small are still deciding what they need.

The market is savage toward the people who lead with tool knowledge, and generous toward the people who understand how a business works. Everyone is polishing the tool names, the model names, the vocabulary. You get hired for walking into a business that doesn't work, seeing why, and making what you build stick.

It is genuinely hard to know what to hire for, and the ground is moving fast enough that you can forgive a company for getting it wrong. But they are starting to figure it out. When I started looking again in May, the job descriptions were more honest than they used to be, asking for a person who understands business process and how to measure the return on it, and fewer of them were the old laundry list of tools nobody who does this work would ever have. That is the signal to watch. A good job description describes a person who exists. A bad one describes nobody, and you do not owe it your self-doubt.

The skill that carries you through it is the one I learned consulting. Sit in a room with people and ask the right questions. Stay curious long enough to understand how the business actually works, how decisions get made and how information flows, where the inertia lives and what the politics are, and who will resist change and why. Find the problem and the cause of the problem before you name a solution, and hold off on building anything until you have synthesized the needs and the constraints and the integrations. You do not need a decades-long career to do it. You have domain expertise, you already know some business from the inside, and that makes you the ideal consultant for that domain, as long as you learn the curiosity and the method. Then you become the person they bring in to solve a meaningful problem and measure the result, someone who can talk to every department and every level without becoming a threat to any of them. Build rapport. Show you understand the constraints they live under, so they believe you can deliver a solution. Because you can.

I make a point of saying this in interviews. What I want is to contribute as part of a team, learn from the data scientists and the developers and the designers, help them upskill, and lean on their expertise to deliver what the company needs. You bring the part that sees the problem and keeps the whole thing aimed at it. The team supplies everything else.

The data backs it. The share of job titles that mention AI tripled in four years, and most of them now live outside tech, in marketing and operations and everywhere the business actually runs. Ask the people doing the hiring what they want and coding comes in dead last, under knowing how to redesign a workflow and how to write down what you did and how to tell whether the output is any good. Their words. Quality control beats coding.

Knowing what good looks like. Looking at what the machine made and saying, no, that is wrong, here is why, here is the proof. Half the people in these roles call it the most important thing they do all day, and it is the hardest thing on the market to hire for. We spent three years asking, can you make it say something clever, and now we are asking, can you show me it is reliable, and that second question is the job.

You still need a base, enough to build with the tools and enough to know when the output is bad, and that bar you can clear from where you sit. What you cannot fake is the other half, understanding the problem and proving the thing works. And you do not have to be a Forward Deployed Engineer to matter. That title is the deep end of the pool, but the same shape runs down into the roles you are actually headed for, the enablement and adoption and product seats, and the fractional builder who parachutes in to fix one thing.

Molly Kinder left Brookings to study what is happening to work, and her word for it is the messy middle, a painful stretch where AI takes tasks and the loss lands hard in concentrated pockets, and for the first time in fifty years the exposed class is the one reading this. Her line is that if you can do your job locked in a closet with a computer, pay attention. Stuart Winter-Tear says it from the other side of the table. The work is redesigning how the business operates, and the agent is just the tool. Read them together and you get both halves of one sentence. She tells you why the ground is moving. He tells you what to stand on.

So here is what I would do. Lead with the research. Before you apply, before you walk in, do the due diligence. Understand what problem they are actually hiring this person to solve, and what they expect you to deliver to make the business work better. Walk in, see what is broken, map how the work actually moves, and only then reach for anything, and sometimes the answer is to build nothing at all. Then prove it. Show up with a mockup, one working thing you built that solves something real, with a before and after you can say out loud. Every real shot I got this summer made me demonstrate. A live build. A working session. A team watching the thing run. Not one was won on vocabulary.

Running around trying every new application and every new agent, one model after the next, buys you practice with tools that will be gone in six months, and nothing else, unless you are using them to solve a real business problem. The breathless announcement that you tried the latest model means nothing to the person deciding whether to hire you. What matters is your domain expertise, your ability to drive change, and, most of all, your ability to measure it. Know the metrics before you start, know why they matter, and what value they carry. Otherwise you get lost in the fantasy that someone will notice you built a hundred-agent swarm managing a company that does not exist.

The thousand-dollar-an-hour "do AI" consultant is already gone, and the people who lasted are the ones who understood the business, because the AI part turned out to be the easy part. The tools got cheap for everyone. The business never did.

And the market is desperate for this, because fifty-nine percent of companies made a bad hire last year, someone who sat there describing an agentic workflow and naming the acronyms and had never once built the thing. The screens let it happen because most of them stop at whether you know the tools exist. A mockup ends that in the first five minutes. You do not have to tell them. They are watching.

The model was never the hard part. The people were always the point.
