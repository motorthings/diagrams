Talk Outline

The hero doesn’t come back the same.

A termination through no fault of my own. A recovery. A new offer — pulled after I disclosed what happened. A legal case running in the background. And in the gap between all of it: building a platform that covered the full lifecycle, a methodology worth teaching, and a way of showing up to an interview that changed the conversation entirely.

There were two losses before the landing. The second one happened because I told the truth. The losses weren’t setbacks on the way to the same destination — they changed what got built. That’s the journey.

Audience: AI Build Lab cohort Format: Talk + Q&A Total time: ~25 min Tone: honest, not a redemption arc — a working story
The Arc
I
The ordinary world
Known quantity
~2 min

Senior AI role at an enterprise content management software company. Building tools, knowing the craft, inside the machine. Good work, meaningful problems, a clear sense of direction. The kind of position that feels stable because it has been stable — until it isn’t.

Talk track
  • Set the scene briefly. What the work was, what it felt like to be doing it well.
  • Don’t oversell the stability. The audience knows what enterprise roles feel like in 2024–2026.
  • One concrete thing you were building that you were proud of.
II
The call
Disruption
~2 min

Termination. Not a performance story — an economic calculation. The company made a decision; the decision landed on you. This beat is short. The temptation is to explain it. Resist. What matters is what happened next, not what caused this.

Talk track
  • Name it plainly. Layoff, termination, restructuring — whatever fits. Don’t over-explain the circumstances.
  • The point is not sympathy. The point is: the ordinary world ended, and the next one hadn’t started yet.
  • One sentence on what it felt like. Then move.
III
The road of trials
Two losses, not one
~4 min

The first loss was the termination — through no fault of my own, a company making a calculation. The second loss was different. A few months later, after recovering and rebuilding, a new offer came in. I disclosed what had happened at the enterprise software company. The offer was pulled. So there were two losses, and the second one happened because I told the truth.

That is the part of this story that would make most people stop disclosing. It would be easy to learn the wrong lesson: keep it vague, protect yourself, let people assume. The audience in this room will face versions of this. Name it directly.

Talk track
  • The first termination: through no fault of your own. A company making an economic decision. This part is common and the audience will recognize it — say it plainly, without self-pity, and move.
  • The recovery: a few months of building, interviewing, showing up. Then an offer.
  • The disclosure: you told the truth about what happened. The offer was pulled. Land on this without editorializing — just say what happened. The audience will feel it.
  • The question it raises: do you stop disclosing? Do you soften it? Do you protect yourself at the cost of honesty? Say out loud that you didn’t. That’s the decision that matters.
  • The market mismatch is real too — AI builders are hard to hire for — but that’s background texture. The two losses in sequence is the trial.
IV
The ordeal
Building anyway
~5 min

After the second loss — the one that happened because of honesty — there is a choice about what kind of person you become. A legal case running in the background. Fully between roles, with no obvious next move. This is the part of the story nobody tells at conferences because it isn’t clean. But this is where the real work happened. No employer to build for, no roadmap to follow, no performance review to optimize toward. Just the actual problems, built the way they should be built, with nothing left to protect.

Talk track
  • Don’t skip the difficulty. The audience respects honesty about the hard part more than a polished pivot story.
  • What got built in this period: AESOP from org context through monitoring. The legal contract review tool. The mockup method emerging as a repeatable process.
  • Why building in the gap produced better work than building inside a role: no product constraints, no stakeholder management, just the problem.
  • The thing that surprised you about what you were capable of when the structure was gone.
  • The legal case: you don’t have to explain it. One sentence is enough. What it taught you about building things that can withstand scrutiny.
V
The reward
The methodology
~5 min

The methodology crystallizes. Not just a platform — a way of working. Research the org until you understand their specific fear. Build on their stack. Seed the artifact with their actual failure modes. Run the presentation as an eval against a voice agent shaped like the people in the room. Know you’re ready when the questions don’t slow you down. This is the elixir in progress.

Talk track
  • Walk through the mockup method at a high level. This is the substance of the talk — give it real time.
  • The Xcel build: researched NY PSC Case 26-M-0552, built a monitor on Databricks, showed up with it running. Got the role.
  • The Melior build: five FakeCo transcripts, a rehearsal agent voiced as the three executives, systematic quote verification. Received an offer.
  • The pattern behind both: their stack, their data, their failure modes. The presentation as an eval. Iterate until the agent’s questions feel slow.
  • What the platform gives you that a one-off build doesn’t: coverage. Any interview angle pulls from something already built and already improving.
VI
The road back
Arriving as a peer
~3 min

The Xcel role. A contract at a regulated utility, owning post-deployment AI quality and governance. The conversation in that room was different from every earlier one — not because the role was better, but because the approach had changed. Showing up with the thing running meant the interview was about what to build next, not whether to trust the candidate.

Talk track
  • What the Xcel room felt like compared to the early interviews. Concrete if you can.
  • The moment it shifted: when they stopped evaluating and started planning.
  • What “arriving as a peer” actually means in practice: you already know their problem well enough to have an opinion on the solution.
  • The honest version: the method doesn’t guarantee anything. It changes the room. You still have to be able to hold the conversation once it opens.
VII
Return with the elixir
Something to teach
~3 min + Q&A

Standing in this room is the return. The elixir is the methodology — portable, improvable, applicable to the next person who is between roles and building anyway. The journey didn’t produce a better version of the old job. It produced a different way of working that compounds across every role from here.

Talk track
  • Name what you’re handing to the room. Not inspiration — a process they can use next week.
  • The three things: deep research on the org and the people, a build on their stack seeded with their failure modes, and a voice agent to run it against before you walk in.
  • The thing that made it work wasn’t the tools. It was the discipline of always coming back to the target: does what I built answer what they asked?
  • What you’re still figuring out. Leave room for the audience to be ahead of you on some of it — that is the honest version of returning with an elixir.
  • Open question to close: what problem are you building toward right now, before you have the job?
The hero’s journey works as a talk structure because the audience already knows the shape. They can feel the arc before you name it. What makes it land is the specificity inside each beat — the actual builds, the actual failures, the actual room. The story isn’t that you survived. It’s what you came back with.
