Two-Phase Authored Discovery

How AESOP moves from mapping what exists to questioning what should exist —
before anything gets built.

Foundation

Organizations are compensating mechanisms for the limits of human intelligence.

Departments exist because people specialise. Hierarchies exist because managers cannot coordinate unlimited work. Meetings exist because context has to move between human beings. Approvals exist because judgement is scarce.

Stuart Winter-Tear, "How Intelligent Systems Change the Design of Organisations" — July 2026

If context becomes cheaper to retrieve, knowledge becomes persistent rather than personal, routine judgement can be delegated, and coordination costs fall — then the structures built around those constraints deserve to be questioned. Most AI deployment just pours intelligence into the existing org chart faster. AESOP's two-phase discovery is designed to do the opposite.

The Fork in the Road

Inherited Discovery

Phase 1 — what most platforms stop at

  • Maps the current process step by step
  • Captures existing roles, approvals, meetings
  • Identifies pain points within the current structure
  • Asks: "How does this work today?"
  • Produces an opportunity brief based on the inherited org chart
  • Fundable, demoable, LinkedIn-postable

Authored Reframing

Phase 2 — what makes AESOP different

  • Questions which roles exist because of old constraints
  • Maps decisions, not departments
  • Separates real constraints from inherited ones
  • Asks: "What would you design from scratch?"
  • Enriches the brief with structural assumptions challenged
  • Scarier, slower, no clean before/after screenshot — and essential

How It Works

Pipeline

Phase 2

Authored Reframing

7 question sections question structural assumptions: Decision Mapping, Context Requirements, Coordination Design, Constraint Identification, Success Redefinition, Structural Assumptions Audit, Autonomy & Accountability.

Each section opens with a context block referencing Phase 1 answers — "You said X. Now question whether X should exist."
→
Output

Enriched Brief

A single markdown document with two sections: Current State (the inherited map) and Authored Reframing (structural assumptions questioned).

Downstream Design → Build pipeline uses this enriched brief unchanged. No new tooling required.

Gateway Decision Point

After Phase 1 compiles, the user sees their brief alongside three paths:

Authored Interview

Structured form with 7 contextualized question sections. Each section references Phase 1 content. 22 total questions across decision mapping, context, coordination, constraints, success, structural assumptions, and autonomy.

Authored Brain Dump

AI-driven free-text entry. An authored gap agent reads the Phase 1 brief plus the user's brain dump and extracts structured authored answers. Gaps are identified and the user fills only what's missing.

Skip to Design

Proceed with Phase 1 only. The standard brief is used as-is. This is always available — not every project needs authored reframing, and forcing it would create friction. But the option is always visible.

Phase 2 Question Architecture
Decision Mapping

4 questions

What decisions need to be made? By whom? With what information? Not what process steps exist today.

Context Requirements

3 questions

What context is required for good decisions? Where does it live — or where should it live?

Coordination Design

3 questions

What needs to happen between people, teams, systems? What does ideal coordination look like?

Constraint Identification

3 questions

Which constraints are real? Which are inherited? What would it take to change each one?

Success Redefinition

3 questions

Describe the ideal end state without current constraints. What is the minimal viable threshold?

Structural Assumptions Audit

3 questions

Which roles, approvals, meetings exist because of tech limits? What would you eliminate?

Autonomy & Accountability

3 questions

Autonomy level per decision. Who is accountable when things go wrong? Audit & feedback loop?

Why This Matters

The replication trap: Most "AI transformation" just pours intelligence into the existing org chart faster. The metric is agent count — a Taylorist instinct applied to something that isn't labor in the old sense. Nobody posts "I eliminated the approval layer that agent count would have made unnecessary." Counting agents answers "what can the technology do" — the boring question. The interesting one is what kind of organization becomes possible when judgement and context stop being scarce and personal.
01

Structure becomes an output, not an input

Inherited discovery treats the org chart as fixed and pours intelligence into its channels. Authored discovery treats the org chart as the thing under question. The difference is what gets designed vs. what gets assumed.

02

Replication vs. dissolution is the real fork

Two entirely different projects hide under "transformation." If removing the agent means going back to the old slow way, you replicated. If the agent's existence makes you realize the role's justification was already gone, you dissolved.

03

Legibility bias drives the replication trap

Automating a standup, a report, an approval chain produces a demo. Standing outside the system and asking "why does this role exist at all" doesn't — it produces a redesign. Scarier, slower, no clean screenshot. And the only thing that's actually transformation.

04

The diagnostic question

Does this agent replace a person, or does it dissolve the reason the role existed in the first place? If you can't answer that, you're automating. If you can, you're transforming. AESOP's two-phase discovery is designed to force that question before a single line of code is written.

If you can answer that, you're authoring the organization. If you can't, you're automating the old one. AESOP's two-phase discovery forces this question before anything gets built.
