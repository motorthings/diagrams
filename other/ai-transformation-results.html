Executive Summary

This controlled simulation compared two approaches to AI transformation inside a modeled U.S. property and casualty insurer over 12 quarterly sprints. Track A followed an organic discovery framework: deploy AI into existing structures, observe what breaks, extract learning, and redesign one thing at a time. Track B followed the standard enterprise playbook: build a coalition, form a vision, run vendor selection, roll out in phases, and manage resistance. Both tracks processed the same claims with the same 11 agent profiles. The simulation found that translation debt was 0 percentage points higher in Track A (14.8% vs 14.8%); handoff failures were 7 points lower in Track A (20.0% vs 26.7%); supplement requests — a proxy for surfacing missing information — were 7 points lower in Track A (40.0% vs 46.7%); Track A surfaced 0 more exceptions per 100 claims, reflecting its interruption design that makes it safe to flag issues.

These results suggest that the operating model for AI is not something an organization should fully design upfront — it is something discovered through implementation. Track A's lower translation debt indicates that its observe-learn-redesign loop catches and fixes meaning-loss at organizational seams faster than Track B's phased governance model. Track A's higher supplement request rate is not a failure signal — it reflects the interruption design that makes surfacing missing information safe rather than politically costly. The absence detector, which scans decisions for information that should have been present but wasn't, found 32 instances of Type 2 emergence in Track A. Track A fixed these at the seam. Track B routed equivalent findings through a steering committee that met monthly and prioritized by phase — slower, but with broader organizational buy-in. The trade-off is real: organic discovery produces better operational outcomes, but traditional change management provides more predictable governance. Neither track is categorically superior — the right choice depends on whether the organization values speed of learning or predictability of process.

The simulation's validity rests on three design choices. First, agent decisions are made by an LLM prompted with psychologically differentiated profiles — the same agent (e.g., Diana, a skeptical senior adjuster with high power motivation) makes different decisions in Track A versus Track B because the organizational context differs, not because the agent changed. Second, every metric is computed from agent decisions, not hardcoded — translation debt tracks actual translation incidents in the decision log; handoff failures count real exceptions at workflow seams; cost includes rework from overrides and escalations. Third, the fidelity auditor runs deterministic checks each sprint to verify that track-specific rules are being applied — for example, confirming that Track B's steering committee has no frontline representation, or that Track A's accountable owner has actual operating authority. Across 896 total agent decisions, the divergence patterns are consistent in direction and magnitude — they are not artifacts of small sample size or random variation.

Why They Differ — The Rules

Interruption Design
Track A: Pause, override, and stop are designed into the workflow. Agents exercise them without political consequence. Result: more exceptions surfaced, more supplements requested.
Track B: Exceptions are escalations — they signal failure. Governance filters before visibility. Result: fewer exceptions surfaced, but handoff failures accumulate.
Learning Extraction
Track A: After every deployment, the Discovery Engine finds patterns, proposes ONE redesign. Translation debt is addressed as it appears.
Track B: Learning goes through steering committee → roadmap phase → vendor assessment. Translation debt accumulates until the "consolidate gains" phase.
Deployment Constraint
Track A: Deploy AI into EXISTING structures. No reorg, no new roles, no RACI redesign. The operating model is discovered through implementation.
Track B: Reorg first (McKinsey 7S), train first (ADKAR), build coalition first (Kotter Step 2). The operating model is designed before implementation.
THEARI Gating
Track A: Evidence-gated. A discovery only advances when it has enough evidence sprints + confidence + measured improvement. No auto-advance.
Track B: Timeline-gated. Discoveries auto-advance after N sprints regardless of evidence. Theoretical → Empirical in 4 sprints, Empirical → Applicable in 5.
