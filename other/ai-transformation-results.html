The Experiment

A controlled simulation compared two approaches to AI transformation inside a modeled U.S. property and casualty insurer over 12 quarterly sprints. Both tracks processed claims, underwriting, and subrogation with the same 13 psychologically differentiated agent profiles. The only difference was the transformation method.

  • Track A — Discovered, Not Designed (SWT): Deploy AI into existing structures. Observe what breaks. Extract learning. Redesign one thing at a time. No reorg, no new roles, no RACI redesign.
  • Track B — Traditional Change Management (Kotter/Prosci): Build coalition. Form vision. Vendor selection. Phased rollout. Resistance management. Steering committee governance.
  • Scale: 10,783 total agent decisions across claims, underwriting, and subrogation domains.

What It Means

The operating model for AI is not something an organization should fully design upfront — it is discovered through implementation.

  • Track A's observe-learn-redesign loop catches and fixes meaning-loss at organizational seams. Agents surfaced 133 missing-information gaps (Type 2 emergence) — decisions made from incomplete information where the incompleteness wasn't recognized at decision time. Track A fixed these at the seam. Track B routed equivalent findings through a steering committee.
  • Track A hits fewer exceptions — its observe-learn-redesign loop fixes the seams that force claims into human handling. Track B's higher exception rate means more claims stall on escalation and rework, not that Track B surfaces more problems.
  • The trade-off is real: the discovered-not-designed approach produces better operational outcomes (lower costs, fewer errors, higher retention), but traditional change management provides more predictable governance (fewer surprises for leadership, higher AI trust scores, more policies bound). The right choice depends on whether your organization needs speed of learning or predictability of process.

Why We Know

Specific evidence from this 10,783-decision run:

  • Agent behavior diverged. Kathryn used AI in 69% of decisions in Track A vs 73% in Track B, and overrode AI 0% vs 0% — the same psychological profile produced different behavior because the organizational rules differed Tommy used AI in 51% of decisions in Track A vs 47% in Track B, and overrode AI 11% vs 11% — the same psychological profile produced different behavior because the organizational rules differed Diana used AI in 7% of decisions in Track A vs 5% in Track B, and overrode AI 0% vs 0% — the same psychological profile produced different behavior because the organizational rules differed
  • Supplement requests: Track A agents requested additional information in 63.2% of decisions vs 88.2% in Track B — a 25.0-point gap. Track A's redesigns complete the seams, so its request rate declines as gaps are fixed; Track B never completes the seams, so its rate stays high.
  • Exception rate: Track A hit exceptions in 14.6% of decisions vs 18.7% in Track B. Track A's redesign loop fixes the seams that force claims into human handling; Track B's higher rate means more claims stall on escalation.
  • Translation debt: Meaning was lost at handoffs in 12.5% of Track A decisions vs 18.8% in Track B. Track A's observe-learn-redesign loop addresses translation seams as they appear. Track B routes them through steering committee → roadmap → vendor assessment.
  • THEARI discoveries: Track A has 133 discoveries, all validated by evidence across multiple sprints. Track B has 10 discoveries that auto-advanced on schedule — same count, zero evidence required.
  • Sample size: Across 10,783 total agent decisions (5,380 Track A, 5,403 Track B), the divergence patterns are consistent in direction across sprints — they are not artifacts of small samples.

Shareholder Impact

Translated to a public-market lens, Track A's approach is worth roughly $7.4B more in market capitalization than Track B — about $12.7 per share (5.9% on PGR's $213 baseline). A directional estimate, not a forecast.

  • Cost channel: Track A handles a claim for $252 vs $346 — $94 cheaper. At 5.5M claims/year that is $517M a year in expense savings, or 0.60 points of combined ratio.
  • Top-line channel: Track A retains 6.5 more points of customers, which compounds into ~2.0 points of premium growth and $265M more annual income.
  • Combined: $782M/year more net income, $1.35 of EPS, and — at 9.4x — $12.7 per share ($7.4B market cap).
  • Caveat: the simulation's absolute cost scale is not calibrated to real dollars, so this is built from the A-vs-B gap (which is defensible), not absolute levels. Anchored to PGR's actual Q2 2026 combined ratio of 87.3%.

Why They Differ — The Rules

Interruption Design
Track A: Pause, override, and stop are designed into the workflow. Agents exercise them without political consequence. Result: more exceptions surfaced, more supplements requested.
Track B: Exceptions are escalations — they signal failure. Governance filters before visibility. Result: fewer exceptions surfaced, but handoff failures accumulate.
Learning Extraction
Track A: After every deployment, the Discovery Engine finds patterns, proposes ONE redesign. Translation debt is addressed as it appears.
Track B: Learning goes through steering committee → roadmap phase → vendor assessment. Translation debt accumulates until the "consolidate gains" phase.
Deployment Constraint
Track A: Deploy AI into EXISTING structures. No reorg, no new roles, no RACI redesign. The operating model is discovered through implementation.
Track B: Reorg first (McKinsey 7S), train first (ADKAR), build coalition first (Kotter Step 2). The operating model is designed before implementation.
THEARI Gating
Track A: Evidence-gated. A discovery only advances when it has enough evidence sprints + confidence + measured improvement. No auto-advance.
Track B: Timeline-gated. Discoveries auto-advance after N sprints regardless of evidence. Theoretical → Empirical in 4 sprints, Empirical → Applicable in 5.
