How the Simulation Works

A plain-English tour. No background needed. If you can follow a game of telephone and a relay race, you can follow all of this.

Written for someone who has never touched the code · every term explained

The whole point

One question, asked fairly

A company decides it wants to start using artificial intelligence to do some of its work. There are two very different ways to go about that. The question we care about is simple: which way ends up with less of a mess?

You can't answer that honestly in the real world, because no two companies are alike. So instead we build a pretend company inside a computer, make an exact copy of it, and run both copies at the same time. One copy adopts AI the first way. The other copy adopts it the second way. Everything else about them is identical: same customers, same staff, same good and bad luck.

Because the two copies are the same in every way except their approach, any difference at the end had to come from the approach. That is the entire trick.

The pretend company

It's an insurance company

We picked an insurance company because its work is easy to picture. Here are the only three ideas you need.

A claim

Someone's car gets dented. They call their insurer and say, "please pay to fix this." That request is a claim. Inside the company, a claim doesn't get handled by one person. It moves from desk to desk: someone checks it, someone estimates the cost, someone approves the payment, someone closes it out.

Underwriting

Deciding whether to insure a new customer in the first place, and what to charge them. Think of it as the company's version of "should we say yes to this person, and for how much?"

Subrogation

A long word for a simple thing: chasing down the other party's insurer to get money back. If the other driver caused the crash, your insurer pays you now and then goes and collects from their insurer later.

That's it. Requests come in, and they travel through a chain of desks until they're finished.

The most important idea on the page

The game of telephone

Every time a piece of work passes from one desk to the next, that handoff is what we call a seam. It's the stitch where two people's work meets.

Now remember the childhood game of telephone. You whisper a sentence to the next person, they whisper it to the next, and by the end it has turned into nonsense. The same thing happens at a seam. The work arrives at the next desk slightly garbled, missing a detail, unclear. So the next person has to stop and untangle it before they can do their own job.

That wasted untangling time is the thing we measure. We give it a name:

The number that decides everything

Translation debt is the extra cleanup work created every time meaning gets lost at a handoff. Less of it is better. Whichever team ends up with less translation debt is the team that ran its company more smoothly. This one number picks the winner.

The two contestants

Two teams, two philosophies

Both teams run an identical copy of the insurance company. The only thing that differs is how they bring AI in.

Team A
Learn as you go

Start tiny. Put AI into one small corner of the work. Watch closely to see what actually breaks. Fix only the things you've seen break with your own eyes. Then, and only then, grow a little bigger.

Careful and grounded, but slow — and blind to any problem it hasn't personally run into yet.

Team B
Big plan first

Do it the way big companies usually do. Pick the technology, design the whole future setup on paper, and roll it out everywhere at once. Manage people's resistance as you go.

Covers a lot of ground fast — but it's all based on a plan, not on watching what really happens.

One team learns by doing. The other plans, then acts. Which produces less mess? That's the race.

One round of the game

What happens in a single round

The simulation runs in rounds. Each round is a stretch of time, like a couple of weeks at the office. Here is everything that happens in one round. The exact same round happens to both teams at the same moment.

1The day's weather is rolled. The computer decides what's going on in the world this round: maybe a flood of new claims, maybe a budget freeze, maybe a quiet stretch. Whatever it is, both teams get the exact same weather. Nobody gets an easier day.
2Each team makes its move. Team A looks at what broke last round and fixes one thing it has real evidence for. Team B follows the next step of its big plan. This is where the two philosophies actually differ.
3The work arrives. A fresh pile of claims lands on both teams' desks — the identical pile for each, so it's a fair comparison.
4Every claim travels its chain of desks. At the desks where AI does the work, the computer tries to handle the claim. Sometimes it works cleanly. Sometimes it "loses the thread" at a handoff, and a human has to step in and sort out the mess. (That mess is the translation debt from earlier.)
5People react. When the AI helps smoothly, the staff trust it a little more. When it hands them garbled work, they trust it a little less. Trust goes up and down based on real experience, not on what anyone was told to feel.
6Some people quit. Staff who get worn down and stop trusting the tools eventually leave. And when one person leaves, it makes their teammates more likely to leave too. Fewer people means more dropped handoffs, which wears down more people. It snowballs.
7Tally the mess. At the end of the round, we measure how much translation debt each team piled up, plus a few other health numbers. Then the next round begins.

Repeat this for many rounds and you get two full stories: how each version of the company fared over time.

A smooth trip costs almost nothing. A garbled one costs real cleanup time and chips away at the staff's trust. Multiply that across thousands of claims over many rounds, and the small differences add up into a clear winner — or a genuine tie.

Why it's a fair test

The referee treats both teams identically

Here's the clever part. Whether the AI succeeds or stumbles on a given handoff is decided the exact same way for both teams, at the exact same moment. If Team A's AI trips over a particular claim, Team B's AI trips over that identical claim too. Neither team gets luckier.

That means any lasting difference between the two companies can only come from how each philosophy fixes its handoffs over time. Not from a good roll of the dice. That's what makes this an honest measuring stick instead of a coin flip.

The catch we spend most of our time chasing

If one team secretly gets a free, permanent fix that the other team can't earn, the fairness quietly breaks — and the test just tells you whatever you were hoping to hear. Finding those hidden thumbs on the scale, and removing them, is most of the real work. A recent audit caught exactly one, and the whole re-run you may have seen me doing is checking the result now that it's gone.

Calling it

How the winner is decided

We let both companies run all the way to the end. Then we ignore the early rounds — every new setup is clumsy at first — and look only at the settled second half.

Whichever team has clearly less translation debt over that settled stretch wins. If they're close enough that you can't confidently tell them apart, we call it an honest tie. We don't crown a winner on a hair's difference, because a hair's difference is just noise.

In one sentence

Build the same company twice, adopt AI two different ways, give both the identical customers and the identical luck, and see which one wastes less effort cleaning up after itself.
